The complete guide to sauna recovery

Sauna therapy is one of the oldest ways to make space for the body to reset. Athletes use it after training. Other people use it to step out of a loud week. Both are welcome here.

Heat, circulation, calm

Heat raises blood flow, loosens tight muscle, and takes the edge off soreness. Heart rate climbs, a little like moderate exercise, which is why sauna time is sometimes called passive cardio. Sweating supports the feeling of a clean reset. The quiet helps too: cortisol tends to drop, and endorphins do some of the mood work.

Traditional and infrared

A traditional sauna heats the air, often around 80–100°C, and encourages a deep sweat. An infrared sauna warms the body more directly, at a gentler room temperature, often around 45–60°C. Both support recovery and stress relief. Infrared suits people who want a softer heat.

How to use it

If you’re new, start with two or three sessions a week, around 15–20 minutes. The best window is after training, not before, so the nervous system can shift from effort into rest. Evenings are popular for sleep. Pairing heat with a short cold plunge — contrast — is how a lot of members build their hour.
